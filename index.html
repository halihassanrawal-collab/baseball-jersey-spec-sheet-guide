Writing a Baseball Jersey Spec Sheet a Factory Can Follow

A clear specification sheet is the difference between a jersey that matches what your committee imagined and one that needs a week of emails to fix. Before you request a quote for custom baseball jerseys, it helps to put every decision on a single page. Factories work from documents, and the more complete yours is, the fewer assumptions they have to make on your behalf.

Short answer: A good spec sheet lists the jersey style, fabric preference, colours with reference codes, logo files, lettering and numbering details, size breakdown, quantities and delivery date. Anything you leave out, the manufacturer has to guess.

Start with the garment itself

Describe the basic build first. Is it a full button-front jersey or a two-button pullover? Short sleeve, raglan or set-in sleeve? Do you want piping along the placket and sleeves, or a clean panel design? If you have a photo of a style you like, include it, but write down what you like about it so the factory knows which details matter.

Note your fabric preference too. If you play in hot weather, ask for a breathable polyester with mesh panels. If you want a heavier, traditional look, say so. You do not need to know technical fabric names; describing the conditions your team plays in is often more useful.

Colours and artwork

Colour causes more disputes than anything else. Screens show colours differently, so give Pantone references if you have them, or send a physical sample of your current kit. For logos, supply vector files such as AI, EPS or PDF where possible. A low-resolution image pulled from a website will need redrawing, which adds time.

Write down:

  • Front chest: team name or logo, its approximate width, and whether it is script, block or arched lettering.
  • Back: player name above or below the number, number height, and font.
  • Sleeves: league patch, flag, sponsor or number.
  • Trim: piping colours, collar and cuff colours, button colour.

Sizes, names and numbers

Attach a roster as a spreadsheet: player name exactly as it should appear, number, and size. Mark youth and adult sizes clearly, since the same label can mean different things. If some players need longer body lengths or different fits, add a column for notes.

Quantities and timing

State the total quantity, including spares, and your required delivery date. Mention whether you will want to reorder the same design in future seasons, because that affects how the factory stores your artwork.

Review before sending

Read the sheet as if you knew nothing about your team. Would a stranger know exactly what to make? Ask a second committee member to check spellings and numbers. Ten minutes of checking at this stage can save a full reprint later.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a designer to create a spec sheet?

No. A clear written description, reference photos and your logo files are usually enough. Many manufacturers will turn your notes into a digital proof for approval.

What file format should our logo be in?

Vector files are best because they scale without losing quality. If you only have a picture, send the largest version you can find and ask whether redrawing is needed.

Should we include sizes in the first enquiry?

A rough size breakdown helps with quoting. The final roster can follow once the design is approved, as long as you meet the deadline agreed with the factory.

Key takeaways

  • Put every decision on one page: style, fabric, colours, artwork, sizes and dates.
  • Use colour references and vector logo files to avoid guesswork.
  • Attach a clean roster with names, numbers and sizes.
  • Have a second person review the sheet before it goes out.

Final thoughts

A spec sheet takes an hour to prepare and saves days of back-and-forth. It also gives you a record to check the proof and the finished jerseys against.
